Redesigning a short-load batch plant to weigh every batch

Short-load plants metered by run time, with no scales, and one ran 7–21% off on cement. I engineered one that weighs every batch and stayed with it in the field until it held 0.31%.

Period
Aug 2025 – present
Company
Cart-Away Concrete Systems
My role
Design, drawings, BOMs, install package, testing, field changes
Team
Controls by the software lead; built by the production team
Tools

What

UCBL2, a short-load concrete batch plant rated at 60 yd³/h, with two 10 yd³ aggregate bins. It weighs cement, sand and rock on load cells, meters water and logs every batch, replacing timed metering that ran 7–21% off on cement.

How

  • Engineered it from design through fabrication: frame, drawings, BOMs, footing and install package.
  • Installed it at a Portland customer in March 2026 and stayed through commissioning.
  • Made 15 field changes from what the run log showed, including a cement diverter and discharge gate I designed and bin-gate flow restrictors.
  • Checked every run against batching tolerances: ±1% cement and water, ±2% sand and rock.

Why it matters

The customer gets specified concrete with a weight record behind every load; every run since July 9 has met all four tolerances. And it repeats: a second plant runs in Montana, two more are in production.

The problem

Cart-Away’s short-load plants have proportioned material by gate opening and run time for 25 years, calibrated by hand. Calibration drifts, timers fault, and nothing records what went into a load. One earlier CBL2 ran 7.0 to 21.1% off on cement across its calibration cycles, with single tests from −22% to +38%.

What I did

  • Weighed design. Rated at 60 yd³/h, with two 10 yd³ aggregate bins. Cement, sand and rock sit on load cells and water is metered; the controller logs target and actual weight of each, every run.
  • Commissioning. Six test batches were rejected before the software lead replaced the original controller on June 3. Then 15 field changes: my cement diverter and air-cylinder discharge gate, bin-gate flow restrictors, skirt boards, a chevron belt where cement hardened on the smooth one, discharge raised from 120 to 150 in., electric bin vibrators, an 80 GPM water pump, a three-belt drive and dust controls.
  • Roll-off mixer. Customized the customer’s 4-yard mixer as a roll-on/roll-off unit for a hook-lift truck.
  • Recycled aggregate. Adapted the recipes to crushed recycled concrete; the customer makes paving blocks from recycled material, with reinforcement recovered from mattresses.
  • Back into the drawings. Every change went into the drawing package used for the next builds.

How I checked it

I reviewed every logged run against published tolerances. The log showed exactly where flow was off: sand and rock ran 5.8 and 6.8% over target until the restrictors went in on June 29, then held at about 0.7%. In August and September all 144 accepted runs passed, and the controller stopped 18 more on missing material.

Accuracy figures are ingredient weights from the controller’s run log, not concrete strength tests.

Commissioning, change by change

  1. The plant left the Salem shop on a flatbed for the customer’s yard in Portland.

  2. Silo, cement auger, aggregate bins, load conveyor and control panel set in place, and an independent electrician made the 480-volt connection. On March 21 the first run, aggregates only, worked as designed.

  3. The tanker put 20 tons of cement into the silo, and dust escaped while it filled. The baghouse and silo pressure relief were adjusted.

  4. The batch came out dry and crumbly in the block form. The water adjustment didn’t work.

  5. On May 22 the belt inspection found cement hardening on the smooth load-conveyor belt at the head pulley.

  6. The original PLC wasn’t communicating with the cement bin. The software lead replaced it with an Arduino-based controller and programmed it, and the plant ran its first successful batch the same day.

  7. The load cells overshot after shutdown. Replacing the communication kit stabilized the readings.

  8. The first block from logged, accepted batches. Earlier hand-cast trial blocks weren’t usable and aren’t counted.

  9. Material overloaded the hopper-to-conveyor transfer and spilled. New skirt boards brought the spillage under control.

  10. Sand and rock had been running 5.8% and 6.8% over target. After the restrictors went in they fell to about 1.3% and 1.4%, and later held at about 0.7%.

  11. A welded bracket raised the hopper belt for clearance over the load conveyor, and a replacement load-conveyor belt was requested.

  12. A chevron belt replaced the smooth one where cement had hardened, and the discharge went from 120 to 150 in. so the customer’s 4-yard mixer could load.

  13. My cement diverter and air-cylinder discharge gate went into service. Every run from this date met all four tolerances.

  14. Electric vibrators replaced the air vibrators to stop material bridging in the bins. The water pump and pipe went from 40 to 80 GPM, and the drive went from two belts to three after the belts overheated.

  15. A rubber discharge chute cut the dust at the truck, the silo pressure relief was replaced, a brighter waterproof 10-in. screen replaced one operators couldn’t read outdoors, and the router was upgraded.

  16. Mechanical issues resolved and the user manual updated. By now the plant had delivered 69 accepted loads, 124.75 yd³, since June 3.

  17. All 144 accepted runs within tolerance, with 0.31% mean cement error. The controller stopped 18 more runs after missing-material warnings.

A modular plant that grows with its owner, and a retrofit kit for plants in the field

The first plant’s lessons went two ways: a next-generation plant that grows one bin at a time, and a kit that brings weighing to plants already working.

Period
Feb 2026 – present
Company
Cart-Away Concrete Systems
My role
Platform design, drawings, supplier selection, bench testing, installation
Team
Software lead on controls; sales staff; outside fabricator and scale supplier
Tools

What

A modular batch plant that starts as a one-bin CBL1 and grows to CBL2, CBL3 and beyond by adding bin modules, and a load-cell retrofit kit for the 400+ older plants still working.

How

  • Traced recurring field failures to root causes with owners: timed metering, cement hardening in augers, spillage at the belt transfer.
  • Designed the modular platform and drew the next-gen CBL2: 96 sheets, released to a steel fabricator for quotation.
  • Specified 35° troughing idlers and a pneumatic clamshell gate to keep material on the belt.
  • Designed the retrofit kit around 10 load cells and bench-tested it for three weeks.

Why it matters

Owners add capacity without replacing a plant, and upgrade the plant they already own instead of buying new. The kit proves itself on Cart-Away’s own test plant before it reaches a customer’s.

The problem

More than 400 older Cart-Away plants are still working, by the company’s estimate. Studying them with their owners, from breakdown photos and on site, I traced the recurring failures to three root causes: timed metering with no weight to check against, cement hardening in augers where moisture gets in, and sand and gravel spilling at the belt transfer where flat rollers can’t cradle it.

What I did

  • Modular platform. The plant grows by adding aggregate-bin modules in line, from CBL1 to CBL2, CBL3 and beyond.
  • Next-gen CBL2 drawings. Main frame, two-bin assembly, and load-conveyor base and frame, with weldment cut lists: 96 sheets between September 13 and October 7, 2026.
  • Belt transfer. 35° troughing idlers with 20° transition idlers, and a pneumatic clamshell gate developed from the UCBL2’s.
  • Beta next-gen CBL1. For this all-electric plant with touch-screen and remote control and scales on hopper and silo: the design changes, new drawing and customer sign-off document.
  • Retrofit kit. Ten 20-tonne load cells for an earlier CBL2, six under its two-hopper frame and four under the silo, with a six-way junction box and wiring. Both hoppers share one frame, so the controller weighs one material, then the other, and subtracts.
  • Level check. Compared hopper level sensors with a supplier, then chose a software check with the software lead: the batch pauses if the conveyor runs but the weight isn’t falling.

How I checked it

I assembled the scales when they arrived on September 2, 2026 and bench-tested them for three weeks for behavior and stability. The first kit is now going onto Cart-Away’s own earlier CBL2. A retrofit goes onto a machine its owner depends on every day, so it reaches customers only after it has proven itself there. The owner of an existing CBL1 was quoted for the kit in September 2026.

A fixed-wing VTOL drone to fly medical supplies in rural Zimbabwe

What

A fixed-wing VTOL drone that carries medical supplies long distances and lands at rough sites with no runway, built from materials and machines available locally.

How

  • Defined the airframe: tapered high wing, 2.60 m span, rotor booms for vertical takeoff.
  • Chose the NACA 4412 over two alternatives for lift-to-drag and a gentle stall.
  • Designed a hybrid structure: CNC carbon-fiber fuselage, printed airfoil sections, foam-filled wing.
  • Checked the wing in FEA (safety factor 1.47), then proved the full VTOL cycle in flight.

The problem

The aircraft had to carry a payload over long distances, take off and land at rough sites with no runway, and be buildable with the materials and machines available locally.

What I did

  • Airframe. A high-wing monoplane with a tapered wing (taper ratio 0.67), 2.60 m span and aspect ratio 10.4, plus rotor booms for vertical takeoff and landing.
  • Airfoil. Compared the NACA 23015, 23012 and 4412 at the design Reynolds number of about 390,000, and chose the 4412 for its lift-to-drag ratio and gentle stall.
  • Structure. CNC-machined carbon-fiber plate for the fuselage, 3D-printed airfoil sections, aluminum at the wing root and XPS foam filling the wing, to balance weight, strength and what we could make.
  • Build. Led prototype manufacturing and assembly, and cut prototyping cost by 18%.

How I checked it

FEA on the wing under flight loads gave a peak von Mises stress of 187 MPa against a 276 MPa yield strength, a safety factor of 1.47. Flight tests then checked stability and endurance through the full cycle: vertical takeoff, transition to wing-borne flight, circuits, hover descent and vertical landing. The prototype was shown at the Zimbabwe Agricultural Show and demonstrated for a Ministry of Health official.

Quadrupling a wave energy converter’s peak power through three prototypes

For my Berkeley M.Eng capstone I led manufacturing on an inflatable wave energy converter, rebuilding it around what each pool test showed.

Period

Organization
TAFLab, UC Berkeley; advisor Prof. M. Reza Alam
My role
Manufacturing lead: design, fabrication, testing
Team
M.Eng capstone team
Tools
Rapid prototyping, 3D printing, power electronics, pool testing

What

An inflatable point absorber with fewer moving parts, aimed at wave power for under $0.20 per kWh.

How

  • Built each prototype’s power take-off and its power electronics.
  • Engineered a printed motor-ring extension that let the generator turn in small waves.

The problem

Wave energy converters stay expensive and hard to keep alive in rough seas. The team set out to show that an inflatable buoy with fewer moving parts could capture useful power at lower cost, working toward a levelized cost of energy under $0.20/kWh.

What I did

I led the build of each prototype’s power take-off, where the mooring line drives a spring-loaded coupler and generator inside a printed shell, and its power electronics: a full-bridge rectifier, a capacitor bank, and regulated 12 V and 5 V outputs. Then we iterated on the buoy:

  1. Ring buoy. Tests in two pools exposed boundary effects in the small pool and motor dry torque holding back rotation. Output rose 36% in the larger pool, to 1.68 W.
  2. Triangular platform on three inflatables. My printed motor-ring extension cut rotational resistance so the generator could turn in small waves, but the body rocked instead of following the wave: 2.4 W peak.
  3. Inflatable buoy with an elevated platform. It followed the wave profile and converted motion far better: 9.7 W peak.

How I checked it

Every prototype went through the same pool test with measured electrical output, so each design change was judged against the last one rather than against a model. The team named three losses for the next stage: dry torque, weak buoy response and no resonance tuning. I presented the poster and took my M.Eng comprehensive examination on the project in May 2025.

A wheel-driven crop sprayer that takes the load off smallholder farmers

Backpack sprayers put 20 litres on a farmer’s back. My capstone moved the tank onto a push frame whose wheel drives the pump. Published at IEEE GHTC 2022.

Period
Aug 2021 – May 2022
Organization
Ashesi University, Ghana; advisor Dr. Heather Beem
My role
Lead engineer: research, design, analysis, fabrication, testing
Team
Individual capstone, workshop support
Tools
SolidWorks, FEA, tube bending, welding, field trials

What

A push-frame sprayer whose wheel drives the pump, specified with nine tobacco farmers in Zimbabwe: under half the effort, automatic pumping, a 50 L tank, under $200.

How

  • Scored four concepts in a Pugh chart; a single wheel with a standard tank won.
  • Sized a steel tube frame for 50 kg and an 18:32 chain drive that pumps 30 strokes a minute at walking pace.
  • Checked the frame and pins with FEA, safety-factor and fatigue calculations, then built it.
  • Ran 29 flow trials and 13 push-force trials on three surfaces.

Why it matters

Farmers push about a third of the load instead of carrying all of it, for $55 in materials against their $200 ceiling.

The problem

Interviews with nine tobacco farmers in Marondera, Zimbabwe set the brief: a push effort under half the load, automatic pumping, a 50-litre tank, adjustable nozzles, and a price under $200, the most they said they would spend.

What I did

  • Concepts. Sketched four and scored them on cost, tank size, effort, fabricability and durability. A single wheel with a standard tank won, because the better-performing irregular tank wasn’t available in Ghana.
  • Sizing. A 13.7 mm OD 1020 steel tube frame for a 50 kg load, and an 18:32-tooth chain drive that rocks the pump lever at 30 strokes per minute at walking pace.
  • Analysis. FEA on the frame, a safety-factor check on the sprocket pin, and a fatigue-life check on the driving pin.
  • Build. Bent and welded the frame and fitted the drive in the university workshop.

How I checked it

29 timed flow trials over a 75 m course, 13 push-force trials on rammed mud, loose sand and lawn, and a lift-force test with a 50 kg load, each compared with my hand calculations. Flow was 1.2 L/min at 1.45 m/s walking pace, within 3.5% of prediction (R² = 0.98), and push effort stayed under the 250 N limit even on loose sand.
